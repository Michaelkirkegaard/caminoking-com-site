Vía de la Plata

The long, lonely trek from south to north for experienced walkers.

The Vía de la Plata runs up through the whole of western Spain, from Seville through Mérida, Cáceres, Salamanca and Zamora, before it bends west to Santiago. It follows an old Roman road, and the ruins along the way are part of the experience.

The stages are long, shade is scarce, and pilgrims are few. That is precisely the charm. If you have a Camino or two behind you and want six weeks with room to think, this is the big one.

History and character

We have walked the Vía de la Plata. We have felt the sun burn on the plains of Andalusia, sensed the silence in the Extremadura dehesa and watched the shadows dance over the high plateaus of Castile. It is more than a route. It is a historic road through the whole of western Spain from south to north.

The route is called the Silver Way, but the name is misleading. It probably comes from the Arabic al-balat, which means paved road, that is a surfaced road from the Roman network. The Romans built it as a trade and military road between Mérida and Astorga, and later it became a pilgrim way for those who came from the south. The ruins along the way are part of the experience.

It is the longest Camino you can walk on well-established trails, around 980 kilometres, and at the same time the one with the fewest pilgrims of the known routes. For us that is exactly what makes it. Fewer people give more quiet, more reflection and more authentic encounters along the way.

Highlights along the way

1

Seville

A magnificent start town with the world's largest Gothic cathedral, the Giralda tower and the Alcázar palace. A grand beginning towards the north.

2

Mérida

One of Europe's best-preserved Roman townscapes with a theatre, amphitheatre and aqueduct. Walking through it is like walking through antiquity.

3

The Extremadura dehesa

The endless oak and cork forest plains with big game and open skies. The silence and calm the route is known for.

4

Salamanca

The golden university city with one of the world's oldest universities and a lavish Plaza Mayor. Lively, young and grand.

5

Castile's high plateaus

Wind-blown expanses through Castilla y León past Zamora and small pilgrim colonies. Mentally and physically demanding, but exceptional.

6

The choice of way north

At Granja de Moreruela the route splits. Carry on north towards the meeting with the Francés, or turn west into Galicia towards Santiago.

How hard is the Vía de la Plata?

The Vía de la Plata is long and hard, mostly because of the climate and the distances. The landscape changes from Andalusia's hot plains and olive trees to Extremadura's dark oak forests and dehesa and on up onto Castile's wind-blown high plateaus. There can be long stretches without shade, so you have to prepare for heat, wind and vast open spaces. You should count on days of up to 25 to 30 kilometres and being more self-sufficient than on the popular routes. The route is marked with scallop shells and yellow arrows in many places, but not everywhere, so a good map or GPS track is an advantage. Make sure you have plenty of water and salt, especially in the south and on the open meseta.

Who does the Vía de la Plata suit?

The Vía de la Plata is for the experienced pilgrim with plenty of time and good fitness. It suits you if you are up for a long and hard route that is not overcrowded, and who thrives on long, empty stretches with room to really think. There will be days with few people and perhaps wind and rain, but that is exactly what gives the route its character. If you are fascinated by Roman history and by the wide open spaces of western Spain, this is the big trek.

How do you get to the start of the Vía de la Plata?

The start town of Seville has an international airport and good train and bus connections, so it is easy to reach from most of Europe. From Denmark the way typically goes with a stopover or via Madrid and onward on the Spanish high-speed train to Seville. Take a day in the city to collect your pilgrim passport, see the magnificent cathedral and walk out of the city early, while it is still cool. Many people, by the way, choose to start in Mérida instead, to make the logistics work better.

How long is the Vía de la Plata?

The Vía de la Plata is about 980 km from Seville to Santiago de Compostela. The sources range from 970.4 to 980.2 km, depending on where they measure from and to, and which variants they count.

Why do the numbers vary?
The route has variants
In several places you can choose between two ways, and they are not the same length. On the Camino Primitivo, Gronze lists the stage from Borres to Berducedo in two versions: 24.1 km via Hospitales and 28.6 km via Pola de Allande. Which way a source counts moves the total by several kilometres. Source: Gronze.
The end point is not the same
The sources do not agree on where the route ends. The Spanish federation of Camino associations puts the town of Fisterra 86.14 km from Santiago but gives 89.4 km for the main route, because it continues out to the cape. The three kilometres or so from the town to the lighthouse explain most of the difference between the low and the high figures for the Camino Finisterre. Source: Federación Española de Asociaciones de Amigos del Camino de Santiago.
The headline figure counts more than one stretch
A figure at the top of a page does not always cover the route you plan to walk. Eroski Consumer gives 939.7 km and 37 stages for the Camino Francés. That is the 31 stages from Saint Jean Pied de Port (775.3 km) added to the six stages from Somport (164.4 km), which is another start of the same route. Source: Eroski Consumer.
The path gets re-routed
The routes are not fixed for all time. Gronze writes that the Xunta de Galicia changed the course of the Camino Inglés between Betanzos and Hospital de Bruma in 2017, and that the stretch became shorter and less steep. A source that has not been measured again after a re-routing therefore shows a different figure from a newer one. Source: Gronze.
Measurement and rounding
Even a single source can have two figures for the same stretch. The Spanish federation gives 112.47 km for the main route from Ferrol next to its GPS track download and calls the figures approximate, while its town list on the same page puts Ferrol 114.98 km from Santiago. The Xunta de Galicia gives 112.4 km in its info box and 112.5 km in the text. When stages are rounded and added up, the sum shifts a little each time. Source: Federación Española de Asociaciones de Amigos del Camino de Santiago.

The stages further down follow Gronze's division, which is why they add up to Gronze's figure.

The route on the map

When should you walk the Vía de la Plata?

Spring and autumn only, in all seriousness. Summer in Extremadura is dangerous walking weather, and most services shut down at the height of summer for exactly that reason.

Frequently asked questions about the Vía de la Plata

How long is the Vía de la Plata, and how long does it take?
The route is around 980 kilometres and takes 38 to 42 days over 37 stages. It is the longest Camino you can walk on well-established trails.
Where does it start and end?
It starts in Seville in the south and runs up through western Spain via Mérida, Cáceres, Salamanca and Zamora, before it bends towards Santiago. Many people choose to start in Mérida instead.
Is the Vía de la Plata hard?
Yes, it is long and hard, mostly because of the climate and the distances. Days of 25 to 30 kilometres, long stretches without shade and few pilgrims call for good fitness and experience.
When is the best time of year?
Spring and autumn only. Summer in Extremadura is dangerous walking weather, and many services shut down at the height of summer.
Roughly how much does it cost?
Count on around 30 to 40 euros a day. On the empty stretches you need to be more self-sufficient than on the popular routes.
Why choose the Vía de la Plata?
For the Roman history, the wide open spaces of western Spain and the quiet. It is the route with the fewest pilgrims, so there is plenty of room to really think.
