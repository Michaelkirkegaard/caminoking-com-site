Camino Finisterre

The continuation to the end of the world, where pilgrims of old walked out to the sea.

Finisterre is the one Camino that turns it all around. It starts in Santiago instead of ending there, and it leads west through the green Galician hinterland out to the Atlantic at Cape Finisterre, the place the Romans called the end of the world.

Many pilgrims carry on here once they have reached the cathedral, to give the journey a real ending by the sea. The roughly 90 kilometres to Finisterre can be extended to about 116 if you also take the beautiful variant out to Muxía.

History and character

The Camino Finisterre is the one route that turns it all around. It differs from all the others by starting in Santiago de Compostela instead of ending there. It was created as an extension, a chance to walk on out to the Galician coast, which the Romans called the end of the world, Finis Terrae.

Many people are bitten by the Camino while they walk it, and when they have finally reached the goal at the cathedral, they are not ready to stop. So they carry on the last barely 90 kilometres out to Cape Finisterre. At the lighthouse the pilgrims stand and watch the sun go down into the Atlantic, and traditionally you mark the end of the journey with a small ritual, symbolically burning a piece of clothing or your worn-out boots.

We have walked out to Finisterre several times, but still miss the pleasure of the last stage to Muxía, where you are said to experience an even more authentic part of Galicia. If you add that variant, the route comes to around 115 kilometres in all. It is a quiet and reflective epilogue to a long walk.

Highlights along the way

1

Setting out from Santiago

Walking out of the cathedral city westward, this time with the sea as the goal, gives the trip a very particular, reversed feeling.

2

Ponte Maceira

An idyllic medieval bridge over the Tambre river, one of the most beautiful and photographed places on the route.

3

First glimpse of the Atlantic

After days in the hinterland the sea appears ahead at Cee and Corcubión. A big moment when the great blue finally lies there.

4

Cape Finisterre and the lighthouse

The route's goal at the end of the world. Here pilgrims watch the sun go down into the sea and mark the journey's end at kilometre stone zero.

5

The Muxía variant

The beautiful extension to the sanctuary at the wind-blown cliffs. Together with Finisterre the trip comes to around 115 kilometres.

How hard is the Camino Finisterre?

Finisterre is a short but genuine route of around 90 kilometres through the hilly Galician hinterland. It rolls up and down through eucalyptus forests, over open ridges and past small villages, before it finally drops down towards the coast. There are some solid climbs along the way, but no real mountains. The last kilometres out to the cape itself run along the sea with a wide view over the Atlantic. The surface is forest paths, gravel roads and cobbles, and the Galician weather can bring rain and damp in every season. In ordinary shape the route is manageable, and the few days give good room to enjoy it slowly and let the journey sink in.

Who does the Camino Finisterre suit?

Finisterre suits you if you have just reached Santiago and are not ready to stop yet. It is for the pilgrim who wants to give their Camino an ending by the sea, watch the sun set at the end of the world and round it all off in a quiet, symbolic way. It also works fine as a short, standalone walk if you only have a few days.

How do you get to the start of the Camino Finisterre?

Since the route starts in Santiago de Compostela, you are already there if you have walked another Camino. If you want to walk Finisterre as a standalone trip, you fly to Santiago's airport and set out from the cathedral. Once you reach Finisterre or Muxía, daily buses run back to Santiago, so you easily get back and onward home.

How long is the Camino Finisterre?

The Camino Finisterre is about 90 km from Santiago de Compostela to Fisterra. The sources range from 86.2 to 90.1 km, depending on where they measure from and to, and which variants they count.

Why do the numbers vary?
The route has variants
In several places you can choose between two ways, and they are not the same length. On the Camino Primitivo, Gronze lists the stage from Borres to Berducedo in two versions: 24.1 km via Hospitales and 28.6 km via Pola de Allande. Which way a source counts moves the total by several kilometres. Source: Gronze.
The end point is not the same
The sources do not agree on where the route ends. The Spanish federation of Camino associations puts the town of Fisterra 86.14 km from Santiago but gives 89.4 km for the main route, because it continues out to the cape. The three kilometres or so from the town to the lighthouse explain most of the difference between the low and the high figures for the Camino Finisterre. Source: Federación Española de Asociaciones de Amigos del Camino de Santiago.
The headline figure counts more than one stretch
A figure at the top of a page does not always cover the route you plan to walk. Eroski Consumer gives 939.7 km and 37 stages for the Camino Francés. That is the 31 stages from Saint Jean Pied de Port (775.3 km) added to the six stages from Somport (164.4 km), which is another start of the same route. Source: Eroski Consumer.
The path gets re-routed
The routes are not fixed for all time. Gronze writes that the Xunta de Galicia changed the course of the Camino Inglés between Betanzos and Hospital de Bruma in 2017, and that the stretch became shorter and less steep. A source that has not been measured again after a re-routing therefore shows a different figure from a newer one. Source: Gronze.
Measurement and rounding
Even a single source can have two figures for the same stretch. The Spanish federation gives 112.47 km for the main route from Ferrol next to its GPS track download and calls the figures approximate, while its town list on the same page puts Ferrol 114.98 km from Santiago. The Xunta de Galicia gives 112.4 km in its info box and 112.5 km in the text. When stages are rounded and added up, the sum shifts a little each time. Source: Federación Española de Asociaciones de Amigos del Camino de Santiago.

The stages further down follow Gronze's division, which is why they add up to Gronze's figure.

When should you walk the Camino Finisterre?

April to October is best, with long days and mild temperatures by the coast. Galicia is green because it rains, so pack proper rain gear whatever the season.

Frequently asked questions about the Camino Finisterre

How long is the Camino Finisterre, and how long does it take?
The route is around 90 kilometres and takes 3 to 4 days. If you add the Muxía variant, it comes to around 115 kilometres.
Why does the Camino Finisterre start in Santiago?
Because it turns it all around. It was created as an extension, a chance to walk on from the cathedral out to the coast once you have reached Santiago on another Camino.
What is the end of the world?
The Romans called Cape Finisterre Finis Terrae, the end of the world. At the lighthouse pilgrims watch the sun go down into the Atlantic and traditionally mark the journey's end at kilometre stone zero.
What is the Muxía variant?
It is a beautiful extension out to the sanctuary at the wind-blown cliffs. Together with Finisterre the trip comes to around 115 kilometres.
Is the Camino Finisterre hard?
It is short but genuine. It rolls up and down through the hilly Galician hinterland with some solid climbs, but no real mountains. In ordinary shape it is manageable.
When is the best time of year?
April to October is best, with long days and mild temperatures by the coast. Pack proper rain gear, since Galicia is green because it rains.
