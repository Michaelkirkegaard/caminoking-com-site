Training for the Camino: an eight-week plan

The Camino takes less fitness than most people think, and it does ask that you have walked with a backpack. Here is an eight-week plan for a normal life.

The Camino takes less fitness than most people believe. It moves slowly, it takes one day at a time, and the body gets used to it along the way.

The short answer to what you need to have done beforehand: walked with the backpack you will carry, in the boots you will wear, several days in a row. That is the exercise that decides the first two weeks on the route, and it has little to do with cardio fitness.

What you are really training

Three things need to adapt, and each has its own pace:

  • The feet. Skin and tendons need to get used to hours in boots with weight on. It takes weeks and cannot be forced.
  • Back and shoulders. The backpack should sit on your hips and off your shoulders, and that feeling should be familiar before you stand with it on the morning of day one.
  • Endurance. It comes fastest of the three and is the one people worry about most.

That is why the plan below is about walking with weight more than about training hard.

Eight weeks, two to four times a week

Weeks 1 and 2. Two walks a week of 5 to 8 kilometres, without a backpack. Ordinary shoes are fine. Notice how your feet react.

Weeks 3 and 4. Three times a week. One walk of 10 to 12 kilometres at the weekend with boots and a backpack of 4 to 5 kilos. Two shorter walks on weekdays.

Weeks 5 and 6. Take the long walk up to 15 to 18 kilometres with a full backpack. Add a shorter walk the day after, even if you do not feel like it. That is where you find out how day two on the Camino feels.

Week 7. The biggest week. Two walks back to back at the weekend, 18 and 15 kilometres, with everything you will carry. Also wear the socks and underwear and bring the plasters you expect to use.

Week 8. Ease off. Short walks, no new shoes, no experiments. The body consolidates what you have done in the previous seven weeks.

Terrain and hills

The Spanish routes are not flat. The Camino Francés begins with a climb over the Pyrenees, and the Camino Primitivo has hills all the way.

Downhill is the hard part. It loads the front of the thigh and the knees far more than uphill does, and it is what people have not trained for. Find a hill, a staircase or a bridge, and walk down it again and again with weight on. And bring trekking poles, because they take a real share of the load on descents.

Twenty minutes of strength, twice a week

It does not need to be more than that, and it can be done on a mat in the living room:

  • Squats, 3 sets of 12
  • Lunges, 3 sets of 10 per leg
  • Hip raises on the floor, 3 sets of 15
  • Plank, 3 times 40 seconds
  • Calf raises on the edge of a step, 3 sets of 20

That covers hips, front of the thigh, back of the thigh and ankles, and it is exactly what a long descent draws on.

Train with what you will carry

The most common mistake is to train light and travel heavy. If you do your training walks with an empty backpack and turn up with 9 kilos, you are training for something other than what you will need.

Pack the real pack, with the weight you expect, and walk with it. Then you find out that the straps need adjusting, that something rubs, and that there are three things you should leave at home. The list is in the Camino packing list, and the weight is easier to keep down once you have felt it on an 18 kilometre walk.

How you know you are ready

You are ready when you can walk 18 kilometres with a full backpack and get up the next day and walk 15 more, without it being a heroic feat. If you can do that, you can walk the Camino.

How many days it then takes depends on the route, and the figures are in how long the Camino is. The food in the last weeks matters more than people expect, and it is covered in eating before the Camino.

Frequently asked questions

How fit do I need to be to walk the Camino?
Less fit than most people believe. The Camino moves slowly, takes one day at a time, and the body gets used to it along the way. What decides how the first two weeks feel is whether you have walked with a backpack before. Whether you can run a half marathon has little to do with it. Feet and tendons need to get used to hours in boots with weight on, and that takes weeks.
How far should I walk in training?
Work up to two walks a week of between 15 and 20 kilometres with exactly the backpack you will carry on the trip. If you can walk 18 kilometres and get up the next day and walk 15 more without it being a heroic feat, you are ready to walk the Camino. It is the ability to repeat the day that counts, more than the single long walk.
Should I do strength training before the Camino?
Two short sessions a week for legs, hips and back make a clear difference, especially on the descents, where the front of the thigh and the knees take the most load. Twenty minutes is enough, and it can be done on a mat in the living room with squats, lunges, hip raises, plank and calf raises. Those are exactly the muscles a long descent from the Pyrenees draws on.
How long should I train before I leave?
Eight weeks is plenty if you already walk regularly. If you have not walked much for a couple of years, give it twelve weeks, and place the longest walks four and three weeks before you leave. The last week should be short and free of experiments, so the body consolidates what you have done instead of having to recover from a new load.
