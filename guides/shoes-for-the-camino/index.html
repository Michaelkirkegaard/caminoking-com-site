Shoes for the Camino: boots, shoes or trail runners

Boots, low hiking shoes or trail runners for the Camino. The surface, pros and cons, membranes, sizing, breaking in, and what research says about blisters.

Camino King · 20 November 2026 · 6 min read

For a Camino in the dry part of the year, low hiking shoes or trail runners are a good starting point. The surface is a mix of asphalt, gravel roads and paths, and the Spanish guide site Gronze recommends light footwear. Boots make most sense with a heavy backpack, on uneven terrain, in wet and muddy periods, or if you have had ankle problems before.

The category matters less than the fit. Choose the pair that fits your foot, has room for your toes, and that you have walked many kilometres in at home. The research on blisters points to two things above all: familiarity with the footwear, and moisture.

The surface on the Camino

There is no official breakdown of the surface. The guide site Stingy Nomads estimates in its 2026 guide that the Camino Francés is about 770 km long, with around 290 km on asphalt and around 480 km on gravel roads and paths. The figures are an estimate from a single guide, but they show the direction: more than a third of the way is on a hard surface.

That matters for the choice. Asphalt and firm gravel call for cushioning and a sole you can stand on for many hours. They rarely call for the stiff sole that boots are built with for uneven terrain.

Boots, hiking shoes or trail runners

The Spanish guide site Gronze recommends low or mid-height hiking shoes that are as light, comfortable and well worn-in as possible. The same site mentions light, high-cut trekking boots as the classic choice when the ankle needs protection. The table is based on the Appalachian Mountain Club’s comparison of the three types.

According to the same source, stability around the ankle comes mostly from the stiffness of the sole, and the high cut helps. The table describes the types in general, and an individual model can differ. If you walk in the cold months, also read about the best time to walk the Camino. If you choose boots, you will find more detail in the guide to hiking boots.

Membrane or breathability

A waterproof membrane blocks water from outside and lets water vapour escape. The Appalachian Mountain Club describes three reasons why feet still get wet in membrane boots:

  • The membrane cannot release sweat as fast as your feet produce it when you exert yourself.
  • The breathability disappears completely once the outside of the boot is saturated with water.
  • Water runs in at the top by the ankle and down through the sock.

The same organisation writes that the membrane makes feet hotter and sweatier in a dry climate, and that it is an advantage in wet and muddy terrain. On a warm, dry Camino the benefit of a membrane is therefore small. In rainy periods it is greater. British health service podiatrists advise against walking in wet shoes and reckon that shoes need 24 hours to dry out fully.

Size and fit

Your feet change size during the day. The podiatrists at Somerset NHS Foundation Trust give this advice, which you can use directly in the shop:

  • There should be about 1 cm between your longest toe and the inside of the shoe.
  • Buy shoes in the afternoon, when your feet may be more swollen.
  • Walk around in the shoes, because the foot gets longer when it bears weight.
  • The heel should sit firmly. If it slips, the shoe does not fit.
  • Bring your own insoles and try the shoes with them inside.

The same guidance points to movement of the foot inside the shoe as the cause of blisters. So a shoe that is too big is no solution either. Try the shoes with the hiking socks you plan to walk in.

Breaking in

A shoe should fit from day one. The Somerset guidance says plainly that a break-in period should not be needed, and that stiff footwear which has to soften puts the feet at risk of injury and blisters. At the same time, the British health service advises wearing new shoes for short periods until they are comfortable.

The research supports the gradual approach. A review in the Journal of Athletic Training from 2024 lists gradual familiarisation with the activity and the footwear among the few preventive measures that are backed by evidence. So wear the shoes on your training walks before the Camino, and walk with a full backpack too.

A second pair for the evening

Gronze recommends a pair of flip-flops or similar, comfortable and quick to dry, for the shower and for walking around town after the stage. That gives your walking shoes time to dry and your feet some air. Choose something light, as it has to be carried in the backpack all the way. See the full Camino packing list for the rest.

Blisters: what the research shows

Blisters form when the skin is exposed to repeated shearing between its layers. Moisture makes it worse. A review in Sports Medicine from 1995 found that moist skin increases friction.

  • Socks: In a 1996 trial with 357 US Marine recruits, 40 per cent got blisters with a thick, dense outer sock over a thin polyester liner sock. With the standard sock the figure was 69 per cent.
  • Tape: In a 2016 trial with 128 runners in a stage race of about 250 km, 98 runners got no blisters where paper tape had been applied. 81 got blisters on untaped areas. An earlier trial showed no effect, so the result is uncertain.
  • Powder and antiperspirant: The reviews from 1995 and 2024 do not find that they prevent blisters.

A systematic review from 2017 gathered 11 controlled trials and concluded that the evidence is scarce. The British health service keeps to the simple things: shoes that fit, moisture-wicking socks, and a change of socks when your feet sweat.

Frequently asked questions

Can you walk the Camino in running shoes or trail runners?
Yes, on a route like the Camino Francés the surface is a mix of asphalt, gravel roads and paths, and the guide site Gronze recommends low or mid-height hiking shoes that are as light and well worn-in as possible. Trail runners come close to that description. They are the lightest type, but the sole is softer and they wear out sooner than hiking shoes and boots. Test them on long walks with a backpack before you travel.
Do the shoes need to be waterproof?
It depends on the season. A waterproof membrane keeps rain and wet grass out, but it cannot release sweat as fast as your feet produce it, and the breathability disappears once the outside is soaked. Water can also run into the shoe at the ankle. In a dry climate the membrane makes feet hotter and sweatier, so its benefit is small in warm months. In wet and muddy periods it is greater.
How much bigger should the shoes be?
There is no fixed rule about a full or half size. Podiatrists in the British health service recommend about 1 cm of space between your longest toe and the inside of the shoe, and trying shoes on in the afternoon, when your feet may be more swollen. Walk around the shop in the shoes, and wear the socks and insoles you plan to use on the Camino.
How long do the shoes need to be broken in?
There is no documented number of kilometres. The research suggests that gradual familiarisation with both the activity and the footwear protects against blisters, and the British health service advises wearing new shoes for short periods until they are comfortable. So start in good time, and do at least a few long walks with a full backpack in the shoes and socks you are taking.
What works best against blisters?
The evidence is thin. A systematic review from 2017 found only 11 controlled trials and concluded that paper tape may work. A newer review from 2024 highlights dense, padded acrylic socks, insoles and gradual familiarisation as the best supported measures. Foot powders and antiperspirants do not appear to help. Change your socks when they are wet, and act at the first sign of a sore spot.
