Blisters on the Camino: how to prevent and treat them

Blisters are the most common reason a Camino ends up shorter than planned. Here are the six things that prevent them, and the treatment that works.

Blisters are the most common reason a Camino ends up shorter than planned. It is rarely injuries, the weather or motivation. It is the feet.

The short answer to how you avoid them: boots that fit and are broken in, socks that can move moisture, tape on the places you know will rub, and a willingness to stop and take your shoe off the second you feel a hot spot.

Why they come

A blister is skin that has had too much friction, too much moisture and too much heat at the same time. Take one of the three away, and you have come a long way.

It also means that a blister is rarely down to bad luck. It is down to a boot that sits crookedly, a sock that has got wet, or a lace that was not tightened after you came down from the Pyrenees.

The six things that prevent them

  • Boots that fit and are broken in. A thumb’s width of room in front of the toes, and break them in before you travel. The NHS advises wearing new shoes for short periods of time until they are comfortable. This is the most important single thing, and it cannot be solved along the way.
  • Socks of wool or a blend. Wool moves moisture and keeps insulating even when it is damp. Cotton holds the moisture against the skin and is the worst option.
  • Two layers, if there is room. A thin liner sock under a wool sock moves the friction from the skin to the space in between.
  • Tape in the morning where you know it rubs. Heel, big toe, little toe, forefoot. Put it on securely, so it stays on. In a trial among ultramarathon runners, paper tape gave 40 per cent fewer blisters on the taped areas.
  • Laces that are adjusted along the way. Tighten over the instep going downhill, so the foot does not slide forward. Loosen on flat stretches, where the feet swell.
  • A change of socks in the middle of the day. A dry pair at 1 pm is the cheapest insurance there is. The used pair dries on the backpack in the meantime.

Stop at the hot spot

The most important habit on the route is also the one people skip. If you feel a hot, stinging spot, stop and take your shoe off straight away.

It takes five minutes. A blister takes five days. Put tape on, straighten the sock, shake the stone out, and walk on. Almost every blister on the Camino could have been avoided by stopping a quarter of an hour earlier.

Treatment once it is there

Small and closed: leave it alone. The skin on top is the best dressing there is. Put a plaster over it that covers the whole blister, preferably a hydrocolloid one, and walk on.

Large, painful and somewhere you tread: here the sources disagree. The NHS advises against bursting a blister yourself and says a doctor can drain a large or painful one. The Danish patient handbook on sundhed.dk describes how to do it yourself if the blister hurts: wash your hands and the blister with soap and water, clean it with disinfectant, make a couple of holes at the edge with a clean needle sterilised with disinfectant, let the fluid run out, and leave the skin in place. Cover it with a plaster or a dressing. If the fluid is mixed with blood, do not pierce it.

Open, where the skin is gone: clean with water and soap, cover with a soft plaster or a padded dressing, and keep an eye on it. Do not peel off loose skin. This is the type that gets infected.

See a doctor if the redness spreads, if the skin turns warm and swollen, if the pain increases, if there is pus, or if you get a fever. If you have diabetes, the risk of infection is higher, so see a doctor early. There is a pharmacy in almost every town on the Spanish routes, and they are used to pilgrims.

The old method of pulling a thread through the blister belongs to another time. None of the sources for this article recommend it.

What to have in your bag

Four things, and together they weigh next to nothing:

  • Sports tape or kinesiology tape, one roll
  • Hydrocolloid plasters in two sizes
  • A couple of sterile needles
  • A small tube of Vaseline or an anti-friction stick

The rest can be bought along the way, and everything can be bought in Spain.

Your feet in the evening

Take your boots off as soon as you are in. Wash your feet, dry them thoroughly between the toes, and leave them in the open air for the rest of the evening. A pair of sandals or flip-flops is the best extra pair of shoes you can bring, and they are on any sensible packing list.

And then the socks. Wash them every evening, hang them up to dry, and always have a dry pair. The choice between the types is covered in walking socks and in the guide to hiking socks for the Camino.

This article is general information and is not medical advice.

Sources

The Danish patient handbook on sundhed.dk, Vabler og gnavsår was used for leaving the blister alone, plasters, draining a painful blister with a sterilised needle, blood in the fluid and signs of infection. American Academy of Dermatology, How to prevent and treat blisters was used for socks, bandages on problem areas, petroleum jelly, washing with soap and water and signs of infection. NHS, Cellulitis was used for fever and for the higher risk with diabetes.

The figure for tape comes from Lipman et al., Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine, 2016, a trial of paper tape on 128 ultramarathon runners. All details were checked on 21 November 2026.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pop a blister?
Small, closed blisters should be left alone, because the skin on top protects against bacteria. On the rest, the sources disagree. The NHS advises against bursting a blister yourself. The Danish patient handbook on sundhed.dk says that a blister that hurts can be drained through holes at the edge with a clean needle sterilised with disinfectant, while the skin stays in place. If there is blood in the fluid, leave it alone. Never cut the skin away, and cover the blister with a plaster afterwards.
What is best against blisters, tape or plasters?
Tape before they come, hydrocolloid plasters after. The American Academy of Dermatology advises putting soft bandages or moleskin on securely in the places you know will rub, and a trial among ultramarathon runners found 40 per cent fewer blisters on areas covered with paper tape. The hydrocolloid plaster is for the blister that came anyway. According to the NHS it protects the blister, eases the pain and helps healing. Follow the instructions on the packet for how long it should stay on.
Does it help to wear two pairs of socks?
For many people it makes a clear difference. A thin liner sock under a wool sock moves the friction from the skin to the layer between the two socks, and it is the friction that makes the blister. In return it needs room in the boot, so try the combination at home on a long training walk rather than starting with it on day one.
When should I take a day off?
When a blister is open and lies somewhere you tread on, or when the skin around it turns red, warm and swollen. A rest day costs one day, while an infected foot can cost the whole walk. If the redness spreads, if there is pus, or if you get a fever, see a doctor rather than walking another stage, and if you have diabetes, see a doctor early. There is a pharmacy in almost every town on the route.
