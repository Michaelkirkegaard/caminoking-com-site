Camino de Santiago routes: seven routes with distances

Seven Camino de Santiago routes compared on distance, stages, terrain and crowds. See which suits a first Camino and how many walked each in 2025.

Camino King · 20 November 2026 · 5 min read
Contents
  1. The seven routes in figures
  2. Route by route
  3. Choose by the time you have
  4. The 100 km rule

If this is your first Camino and you are unsure, choose the Camino Francés. It is the route with the most albergues and services, it is the one most often recommended to first-time pilgrims, and 46 per cent of all pilgrims walked it in 2025.

With only one to two weeks, the Camino Portugués from Porto or Tui or the short Camino Inglés fits better. For mountains and fewer people, look at the Camino Primitivo or the Camino del Norte. The Vía de la Plata is for those who want to walk far in peace and quiet, and the Camino Finisterre is an extension out to the sea after Santiago.

Route by route

Camino Francés

The Camino Francés runs from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port over the Pyrenees and across northern Spain. It has the most infrastructure and the most places to stay, so days can be made short or long as needed. The first stage is one of the hardest, with more than 1,200 metres of ascent to the Lepoeder pass at 1,432 metres. The drawback is crowding, especially on the last 100 km from Sarria, where around 31 per cent of all pilgrims started in 2025.

Camino Portugués, central and coastal

The Camino Portugués is the second most walked route. Correos describes it as low to medium in difficulty, with regular terrain and mostly asphalt and dirt tracks. The central route runs inland and is demanding in places. The coastal route follows the Atlantic on promenades, wooden walkways and dirt roads and is more relaxed. It grew by 20 per cent in 2025. From Lisbon the route is about 600 km in 25 stages, and from Tui there are 6 stages to Santiago.

Camino del Norte

The Camino del Norte follows Spain’s north coast from Irún through the Basque Country, Cantabria and Asturias. The hardest stages are in the Basque Country. In Cantabria the terrain is easier, but much of the way is on asphalt, and the final stages in Galicia are far from the sea. The route alternates between built-up tourist areas and stretches of great natural beauty. Choose it if you have five weeks and want the sea and fewer pilgrims than on the Francés.

Camino Primitivo

The Camino Primitivo runs from Oviedo through the mountains of Asturias and joins the Camino Francés in Melide. Correos calls it one of the hardest Camino routes, with climbs and descents all the way and muddy paths. Gronze describes the number of pilgrims as still moderate, but the route grew by 14 per cent in 2025. It suits you if you are fit and have two weeks.

Camino Inglés

The Camino Inglés from Ferrol is short, pleasant and well marked, and it is getting busier. At about 115 km it is just long enough for a Compostela, and it fits into a week’s holiday. The variant from A Coruña is about 72 km in 3 stages.

Vía de la Plata

The Vía de la Plata from Seville is the longest of the routes. Distances between towns are enormous, services are fewer and summer temperatures are extreme. Correos advises against July, August and September and recommends March, April, May and October. It is a route for the experienced walker looking for quiet.

Camino Finisterre

The Camino Finisterre starts in Santiago and runs to Fisterra in about 90 km and 4 stages, or to Muxía in 3 stages. You can collect the Fisterrana and Muxiana certificates, but not a Compostela, unless you walk the opposite way into Santiago and cover at least 100 km. The route works well as a finale after another Camino.

Choose by the time you have

  • About one week: the Camino Inglés from Ferrol, the Camino Portugués from Tui or the last 100 km from Sarria.
  • About two weeks: the Camino Portugués from Porto or the Camino Primitivo.
  • Five weeks: the whole Camino Francés or the Camino del Norte.
  • Six weeks or more: the Vía de la Plata.

The season matters for the choice too. See when to walk the Camino before you decide.

The 100 km rule

For a Compostela you have to walk at least 100 continuous kilometres on the same route, including the final stage into the cathedral. By bicycle the requirement is 200 km. That is why many people start in Sarria, Tui or Ferrol, which are a little over 100 km from Santiago.

Sources

Pilgrim figures per route in 2025 come from the statistics of the Oficina de Acogida al Peregrino, as reproduced by CaminoWays and American Pilgrims on the Camino. The rules for the Compostela are from the pilgrim office page on the Compostela.

The lengths are rounded from the sources on each route page, and the stages are Gronze's. Terrain descriptions are from El Camino con Correos and its pages on the individual routes, supplemented by the route guides at Gronze. Elevation figures for the first stage of the Camino Francés are from the Gobierno de Navarra. Figures checked on 20 November 2026.

Frequently asked questions

Which Camino route is best for beginners?
The Camino Francés is the safe choice for a first Camino. It has the most albergues and services, is well marked and is easy to split into shorter sections. The Spanish postal service Correos calls it the route most often recommended to first-time pilgrims. The price is crowding: 46 per cent of all pilgrims walked the Camino Francés in 2025, and the guide site Gronze names crowding as the route's biggest drawback.
Which Camino route is the shortest?
The Camino Inglés from Ferrol is the shortest route into Santiago in this overview, at about 115 km in 5 stages. The Camino Finisterre is shorter still at about 90 km, but it starts in Santiago and heads out to the coast. You can also walk a short section of a long route, such as the Camino Portugués from Tui in 6 stages or the last 100 km of the Camino Francés from Sarria.
Which route has the fewest pilgrims?
Of the seven routes here, the Vía de la Plata had the fewest in 2025, with about 9,000 pilgrims or 2 per cent. The Camino del Norte had about 21,000 and the Camino Primitivo about 28,000. By comparison, about 242,000 walked the Camino Francés. The figures count pilgrims who received their Compostela in Santiago, so they say most about crowding on the final stages.
How far do you have to walk to get a Compostela?
You have to walk at least 100 continuous kilometres on the same route, including the final stage into the cathedral in Santiago. By bicycle the requirement is at least 200 kilometres. The route has to be one recognised by the pilgrim office. That is why many people start in Sarria, Tui or Ferrol, all a little over 100 km from Santiago. The A Coruña variant of the Camino Inglés is only about 72 km.
What is the difference between the central and coastal Camino Portugués?
Both start in Porto and meet in Galicia. The central route runs inland for about 240 km in 10 stages and is described as demanding in places. The coastal route is about 270 km in 13 stages and follows the Atlantic on promenades, wooden walkways and dirt roads. Correos describes the coastal route as more relaxed. In 2025 about 101,000 walked the central route and about 90,000 the coastal route.
