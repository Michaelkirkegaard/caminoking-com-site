Camino Portugués from Porto: central or coastal route

Porto to Santiago: the central route, the coastal route, the Senda Litoral and the Variante Espiritual, with lengths, stages, borders and pilgrim numbers.

Camino King · 21 November 2026 · 8 min read

Two main ways lead from Porto to Santiago. The central route runs inland and crosses the border at Valença and Tui. The coastal route, the Caminho Português da Costa, follows the Atlantic and reaches Galicia at A Guarda. They meet in Redondela and share the last 83.9 kilometres.

The central route is the shorter one: 241.4 kilometres in 10 stages against 270.9 kilometres in 13 stages for the coastal route, when Gronze’s stages are added up. In 2025 the Pilgrim’s Reception Office in Santiago registered 100,803 pilgrims on the central route and 89,503 on the coastal route. The whole route from Lisbon is described on the route page for the Camino Portugués.

The sources do not fully agree. The Spanish postal service Correos gives about 240 kilometres and 11 stages for the central route and about 280 kilometres and 13 stages for the coastal route. The hiking blog Stingy Nomads measures the coastal route at 274 kilometres and the Senda Litoral at 280. One stage equals one day if you follow the guide. The Pilgrim’s Reception Office states that people cover an average of 25 kilometres a day on foot.

The Variante Espiritual is a detour between Pontevedra and Padrón that can be added to either the central route or the coastal route. It does not start in Porto.

The central route

The central route is the part of the Camino Portugués that comes up from Lisbon. Gronze’s stages from Porto pass Vilarinho, Barcelos, Ponte de Lima and Rubiães on the way to Tui, 125.6 kilometres in all, and from there Redondela, Pontevedra, Caldas de Reis and Padrón on the way to Santiago, 115.8 kilometres in all. The longest stage is Barcelos to Ponte de Lima at 34.5 kilometres.

Correos describes the route as beautiful but demanding in some places, and the Camino Portugués as a whole as a route of medium to low difficulty on asphalt and dirt tracks.

The coastal route and the Senda Litoral

According to Gronze, the coastal route follows the Atlantic from Porto through Viana do Castelo, A Guarda and Vigo to Redondela. Gronze’s stages are Porto, Labruge, Póvoa de Varzim, Marinhas, Viana do Castelo, Caminha, Porto Mougás, A Ramallosa, Vigo and Redondela, 187 kilometres in all. Correos calls the route more relaxed than the central one and writes that it mostly runs on promenades, wooden walkways and dirt roads.

The ten Portuguese municipalities behind the website Caminho Português da Costa, from Porto to Valença, give 149.5 kilometres and an average of seven days for their part of the route.

The Senda Litoral is the way that stays by the water wherever possible. Stingy Nomads describes it and the coastal route as sharing the same start and end point on each stage, so you can switch between them, the coastal route as well marked with yellow arrows, and the Senda Litoral as inconsistently marked. The difference is greatest on the way out of Porto. Gronze writes that you can follow the river Douro and the beaches or take the inland way past Padrão da Légua and the airport, where you never see the sea, and that most pilgrims choose the river and the beaches.

The border: Valença and Tui, or A Guarda

On the central route, according to Correos, you cross the International Bridge over the river Miño after 125 kilometres. Valença do Minho lies on the Portuguese side, and Tui is the first town in Galicia.

On the coastal route, according to the Xunta de Galicia, you enter Galicia at the seafaring town of A Guarda after crossing the mouth of the Miño, and Correos writes that the Portuguese part of the coastal route ends with a ferry across the river. No official source could be found to confirm whether the ferry between Caminha and A Guarda is running at present, so check the crossing locally before you plan the day. Gronze also lists a 32 kilometre link stage from Caminha along the river to Tui, where you join the central route.

The Variante Espiritual

According to Gronze, the variant begins 3.1 kilometres after Pontevedra. The first part runs to the monastery of San Xoán de Poio and the fishing village of Combarro, and the second climbs through the wooded hills of O Salnés to the monastery of Armenteira. Gronze’s three stages are Pontevedra to A Armenteira at 20.5 kilometres, on to Vilanova de Arousa at 23.4 kilometres, and from there to Padrón at 35 kilometres.

According to Gronze, the last stage can be replaced by a boat trip from Vilanova de Arousa along the route known as the Translatio. Gronze calls the crossing beautiful but expensive and gives no price. The Xunta de Galicia describes the sea and river route across the Ría de Arousa and up the river Ulla as a commemoration of the tradition that the body of the Apostle James arrived in Galicia by sea. Whether the boat trip counts towards the Compostela is not stated in the sources read, so ask the Pilgrim’s Reception Office if you are walking close to the minimum distance.

Together the two Portuguese routes had 190,306 pilgrims, which is about 36 per cent of the total. Only the Camino Francés was larger, with 241,995.

The 100 kilometres and the short starts

The Pilgrim’s Reception Office requires at least 100 continuous kilometres on foot on the same route, and the final stage must lead into the cathedral. If you started outside Spain, at least 70 kilometres must be walked in Spain. On the last 100 kilometres the pilgrim passport needs at least two stamps a day. Many people therefore walk only the last part.

The statistics list Porto as Oporto with 26,483, Oporto Costa with 25,087 and Porto (por Tui) with 20,772 pilgrims. From Vigo, Gronze’s stages add up to slightly under 100 kilometres, while Correos gives 100 kilometres and writes that the distance earns the Compostela. If you start in Vigo, it is therefore wise to have the distance confirmed by the Pilgrim’s Reception Office. Gronze notes that the Xunta’s hostel in Tui gives priority to pilgrims arriving on foot from Portugal. The 100 kilometre rule is the same as on the Camino Francés, which is described in the guide to the last 100 km from Sarria.

How to get to Porto

Porto has its own airport. Metro do Porto states that line E, the violet line, connects the airport station with Trindade in the city. According to Stingy Nomads, both routes begin at the cathedral, the Sé. Flights, trains and buses to the other starting towns are gathered in the guide to getting to the Camino.

To compare with the other ways to Santiago, see the guide to choosing a Camino route, and read about the seasons in the guide to the best time to walk the Camino.

The pilgrim numbers were read from the Pilgrim's Reception Office statistics for 2025, and the rules come from The Compostela and the office's questions and answers.

Frequently asked questions

How long is the Camino Portugués from Porto to Santiago?
It depends on the way you choose. Adding up Gronze's stages, the central route is 241.4 kilometres in 10 stages and the coastal route 270.9 kilometres in 13 stages. The Spanish postal service Correos gives about 240 kilometres and 11 stages for the central route and about 280 kilometres and 13 stages for the coastal route.
What is the difference between the central route and the coastal route?
The central route runs inland through Barcelos and Ponte de Lima and crosses the border between Valença and Tui. The coastal route follows the Atlantic through Viana do Castelo, reaches Galicia at A Guarda and continues through Baiona and Vigo. Correos describes the central route as beautiful but demanding in places, and the coastal route as more relaxed, on promenades, wooden walkways and dirt roads. The two meet in Redondela.
Where do the coastal route and the central route meet?
In Redondela in Galicia. Gronze writes that the Camino de la Costa joins the Camino Portugués Central on entering the town, and the Xunta de Galicia says the same. From Redondela it is 83.9 kilometres to Santiago by Gronze's stages, and that stretch is shared by everyone coming from Porto.
What is the Variante Espiritual?
It is a variant between Pontevedra and Padrón. According to Gronze it begins 3.1 kilometres after Pontevedra, passes the monastery of Poio, the fishing village of Combarro and the monastery of Armenteira on the way to Vilanova de Arousa, and is 78.9 kilometres in three stages. The main route between the same two towns is 39.7 kilometres. The last stage can be replaced by a boat trip from Vilanova de Arousa.
Can you get the Compostela if you start in Tui or Vigo?
The Pilgrim's Reception Office requires at least 100 continuous kilometres on foot on the same route. From Tui it is 115.8 kilometres by Gronze's stages and 118.8 kilometres according to the Xunta de Galicia. From Vigo, Gronze's stages give 99.3 kilometres, while Correos gives 100 kilometres and writes that the distance earns the Compostela. In 2025 the office registered 41,643 pilgrims from Tui and 18,678 from Vigo.
How do you get from Porto airport to the city?
By metro. Metro do Porto states that line E, the violet line, connects the airport station with Trindade station in the city. Journey time and fare were not found in a current official source, so check them with Metro do Porto before you travel.
