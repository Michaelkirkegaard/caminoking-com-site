Camino Francés

The classic route, and the one most people mean when they say the Camino.

The Francés is the Camino from the books and the films. It crosses the Pyrenees on the first day and then rolls on through Pamplona, the vineyards of Rioja, the wide meseta after Burgos and the mountains of León, before the green hills of Galicia carry you into Santiago.

It is the route with the most pilgrims, the most albergues and the strongest infrastructure. If this is your first Camino and you want the full experience, arrows everywhere, company when you want it and quiet when you need it, then this is the one.

History and character

When we set off on the Camino Francés for the first time, we knew nothing about the Camino. We had a cheap and far too heavy backpack and a pair of untested hiking boots, and it still turned out to be the best month of our lives. When people ask us when we have been happiest, we still answer without hesitation: the Camino Francés.

It is the Camino from the books and the films, the one most people mean when they simply say the Camino. Originally it is a pilgrimage to the tomb of the apostle James in Santiago de Compostela, but you do not need to be Christian or spiritual to have wonderful experiences. For us it was as much about the people we met as about the road itself. You come away richer in stories, not only about your own life but about other people's too.

What makes the Francés special is how civilised it is. Countless books and films have been made about the route, and along the way you find just about anything your heart desires. The hospitality was overwhelming every time we arrived somewhere new, and even when a place was overbooked, we found somewhere to sleep. After a couple of weeks most people dream of yellow arrows and scallop shells, that is how well it is waymarked.

Highlights along the way

1

The Pyrenees and Roncesvalles

The first day's climb from France over the mountains to the monastery at Roncesvalles. A hard start, but one of the most beautiful stages on the whole route.

2

Pamplona

The first real city, famous for the running of the bulls. Tapas bars, a lively old town and a good place to celebrate the first week.

3

The vineyards of Rioja

Rolling wine country around Logroño and Nájera, where the path winds between the rows. Some wineries even have a pilgrim fountain that pours wine.

4

The meseta

The great high plateau between Burgos and León. Open skies, grain fields to the horizon and long, meditative days that many people end up loving.

5

Cruz de Ferro

The iron cross in the mountains near Foncebadón, where pilgrims lay a stone brought from home. The route's most symbolic stop and a quiet moment for many.

6

O Cebreiro and Galicia

The green climb into Galicia with thatched Celtic houses and mist over the valleys. From here Santiago feels close.

How hard is the Camino Francés?

When you walk across Spain, you meet all of it. The terrain is hugely varied, from the mountain crossing over the Pyrenees on the first day to gravel tracks, tarmac, forest and the wide meseta after Burgos. It is a cross country trek in the best sense of the words. Finding your way, on the other hand, is easy. The route is so well marked with yellow arrows and scallop shells that most people quickly find their way back if they stray a little. The difficulty lies mostly in the total distance. We walked the route in 26 days, but if you follow the official stages, you get a gentler pace. The individual days are manageable for most people in ordinary shape.

Who does the Camino Francés suit?

If this is your first Camino and you want the full experience with company when you want it, and quiet when you need it, then this is the one. Our best advice, though, is to avoid being too set in your expectations. We have seen that the people who struggle on the Camino are often the ones who have prepared so much that the route cannot live up to the picture in their head. If you have decided in advance that the trip has to change your whole life, you can end up spoiling your own anticipation. Walk with an open mind, and the Francés delivers the rest.

How do you get to the start of the Camino Francés?

Most people travel to Biarritz or Bayonne in France and from there take the little regional train or a shuttle bus up to Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port. From Denmark the simplest way is usually a flight to Biarritz or Bilbao and onward by train and bus. Arrive in Saint-Jean the day before, so you can collect your pilgrim passport and start the Pyrenees stage rested and early the next morning.

How long is the Camino Francés?

The Camino Francés is about 770 km from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port to Santiago de Compostela. The sources range from 763.7 to 775.3 km, depending on where they measure from and to, and which variants they count.

Why do the numbers vary?
The route has variants
In several places you can choose between two ways, and they are not the same length. On the Camino Primitivo, Gronze lists the stage from Borres to Berducedo in two versions: 24.1 km via Hospitales and 28.6 km via Pola de Allande. Which way a source counts moves the total by several kilometres. Source: Gronze.
The end point is not the same
The sources do not agree on where the route ends. The Spanish federation of Camino associations puts the town of Fisterra 86.14 km from Santiago but gives 89.4 km for the main route, because it continues out to the cape. The three kilometres or so from the town to the lighthouse explain most of the difference between the low and the high figures for the Camino Finisterre. Source: Federación Española de Asociaciones de Amigos del Camino de Santiago.
The headline figure counts more than one stretch
A figure at the top of a page does not always cover the route you plan to walk. Eroski Consumer gives 939.7 km and 37 stages for the Camino Francés. That is the 31 stages from Saint Jean Pied de Port (775.3 km) added to the six stages from Somport (164.4 km), which is another start of the same route. Source: Eroski Consumer.
The path gets re-routed
The routes are not fixed for all time. Gronze writes that the Xunta de Galicia changed the course of the Camino Inglés between Betanzos and Hospital de Bruma in 2017, and that the stretch became shorter and less steep. A source that has not been measured again after a re-routing therefore shows a different figure from a newer one. Source: Gronze.
Measurement and rounding
Even a single source can have two figures for the same stretch. The Spanish federation gives 112.47 km for the main route from Ferrol next to its GPS track download and calls the figures approximate, while its town list on the same page puts Ferrol 114.98 km from Santiago. The Xunta de Galicia gives 112.4 km in its info box and 112.5 km in the text. When stages are rounded and added up, the sum shifts a little each time. Source: Federación Española de Asociaciones de Amigos del Camino de Santiago.

The stages further down follow Gronze's division, which is why they add up to Gronze's figure.

Budget for the whole walk

With public albergues and pilgrim menus, most pilgrims land around 30 to 40 euros a day: a bed typically costs 7 to 16 euros, a pilgrim menu around 10 to 12 euros, and the rest goes on lunch, coffee and the occasional treat. A full five week Camino Francés can therefore be done for roughly 1,300 to 1,500 euros plus the flight, and cheaper if you cook for yourself.

The route on the map

When should you walk the Camino Francés?

May, June and September are the best months: long days, open albergues and gentler heat than in July and August. Winter is possible, but quiet, and a few high stretches demand respect.

Frequently asked questions about the Camino Francés

How long is the Camino Francés, and how long does it take?
The route is around 770 kilometres and usually takes 31 to 35 days over 33 classic stages. We walked it ourselves in 26 days, but if you follow the official stages, you get a gentler pace.
Where does the route start and end?
It starts in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in France, crosses the Pyrenees on the first day and ends in Santiago de Compostela.
Is the Camino Francés suitable for beginners?
Yes. It is so well marked with yellow arrows and scallop shells and has the most albergues and the strongest infrastructure that it is the obvious first Camino. The difficulty lies mostly in the total distance.
When is the best time of year?
May, June and September are best, with long days, open albergues and gentler heat than in July and August.
Roughly how much does it cost?
If you sleep in albergues and eat pilgrim menus, you can count on around 30 to 40 euros a day.
Why choose the Camino Francés over the other routes?
It is the most famous and most civilised route, with company when you want it and quiet when you need it. If you want the full Camino experience, this is the one.
